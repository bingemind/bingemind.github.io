How the comparison works

A fair comparison looks at net worth, not at monthly payments alone.

If you buy

You pay the deposit and buying costs, then the mortgage, property tax, insurance and maintenance each month. At the end you sell: your wealth is the home's value, minus selling costs, minus the mortgage still owed.

If you rent

You keep the deposit and buying costs and invest them. Each month you pay rent, which rises every year.

Investing the difference

Whichever option costs less in a given month, the saving is invested at the return you enter. This matters: in the early years renting is usually cheaper month to month, and in later years owning often is, because rent rises and a fixed mortgage payment does not.

Wealth if buying = home value × (1 − selling costs) − mortgage balance + invested savings
Wealth if renting = invested deposit and buying costs + invested monthly savings

Why how long you stay decides it

Buying and selling a home is expensive. Fees, taxes and agent commission often total 8% to 10% of the price over a round trip. In the first years those costs are larger than the equity you build, so renting usually wins for short stays.

The result shows the year buying starts to win. In many markets that is somewhere between 4 and 8 years, but it depends heavily on:

  • The price-to-rent ratio. Divide the home price by a year of rent. Below about 15, buying tends to win sooner. Above 20, renting is often the better deal.
  • Home price growth compared with investment returns. Try a lower growth figure to see how sensitive your result is.
  • The mortgage rate. Higher rates push the break-even year later.

Costs of owning that people forget

  • Maintenance and repairs: about 1% of the home's value a year on average, and more for older homes.
  • Property tax or council tax: owners pay it directly. In the UK, tenants usually pay council tax too, so set it to 0 if it is the same either way.
  • Insurance: buildings cover is the owner's cost.
  • Buying costs: legal fees, surveys, lender fees, and stamp duty or land transfer tax where they apply. These can be several percent of the price.
  • Selling costs: agent commission is around 5% to 6% in the US and 1% to 3% in the UK, Australia and India, plus legal fees.
  • The deposit's lost return: money in the house is not earning in the market. The calculator counts this by investing it on the rent side.

What the numbers cannot tell you

Some reasons to buy or rent are not financial:

  • Stability: an owner cannot be asked to leave, and a fixed-rate payment does not rise with rents.
  • Flexibility: a renter can move for a job in weeks, and is not exposed to a fall in local prices.
  • Forced saving: a mortgage makes you build equity every month. Renting only wins on paper if you actually invest the difference.
  • Control: owners can renovate, keep pets and stay as long as they like.

Use the calculator to see what the choice costs or earns you, then weigh that against what matters to your household.

Rent or buy questions

Is it better to rent or buy a home?

It depends mainly on how long you stay, the price compared with local rents, and the mortgage rate. Buying usually wins for stays longer than about five to seven years, and renting for shorter ones. Enter your own numbers to see your break-even year.

What is the 5% rule for rent vs buy?

A rough test: multiply the home price by 5% and divide by 12. If you can rent a similar home for less than that each month, renting is likely the cheaper choice. The 5% stands for property tax, maintenance and the cost of capital.

What is a good price-to-rent ratio?

Divide the price by one year of rent. Under about 15 favours buying, 15 to 20 is a close call, and over 20 favours renting.

Is renting throwing money away?

No. Rent buys a place to live, just as mortgage interest, property tax, insurance and repairs do for an owner. Only the principal part of a mortgage payment builds equity.

Does the calculator include tax relief on mortgage interest?

No. Tax treatment differs by country and by household, and in the US most people now take the standard deduction. If you do get relief, buying is slightly better than shown.

What return should I assume on investments?

A long-run figure of 5% to 7% a year for a diversified portfolio is common. Use a lower figure if you would keep the money in savings.
