How to talk like Shakespeare

Type a short modern sentence above. The tool gives one Shakespeare-style version and a reason for each changed word. It uses checked words and grammar patterns. It will not invent a speech, add a greeting you did not type, or make the rhythm of a sonnet.

Start with a familiar greeting: “Hello, how are you?” becomes “Good morrow, how art thou?” Hello changes to a morning greeting, so choose a different phrase if the time matters. Goodbye becomes farewell. Yes becomes ay. No, as an answer, becomes nay.

Use thou for the subject, thee for the object

“You have my book” becomes “Thou hast my book”. In “I trust you”, you is an object, so the style form is thee. Your becomes thy before most words and thine before a vowel sound. “Your eyes” becomes “thine eyes”. Yours becomes thine.

This style uses singular, familiar thou and thee. Shakespeare also used you for formal address and for more than one person. The rules keep a clear plural such as “you both”. They leave uncertain uses for you to check, since a short sentence can have more than one reading.

Check the result in context

Read the notes under every line. A form that looks old is not always right: a noun such as book should never become booketh. The tool uses known verbs and keeps unknown forms. The optional “Put not after the verb” setting changes supported phrases such as “I do not know” into “I know not”.

Use this for a card, a greeting or a grammar exercise. For a line from a play, switch directions or open the Shakespeare to modern English translator. Teachers can hide answers, print a study sheet or make a worksheet with a separate answer key.
