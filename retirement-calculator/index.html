How the retirement numbers are worked out

The calculator answers two questions and compares them.

1. What will you have?

Your current savings and monthly contributions grow at the return you enter until the day you retire. Include any employer match in the monthly amount.

2. What do you need?

You enter the yearly income you want in today's money, and any pension or social security you expect. The difference has to come from savings. That amount is increased with inflation up to your retirement date, then withdrawn every year, rising with inflation, until the age you plan to.

Nest egg needed = W × (1 − (1 + g)−N) ÷ g × (1 + g)
W = first-year withdrawal · N = years in retirement · g = real return = (1 + return) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1

If what you will have is less than what you need, the result shows the extra amount to save each month from now to close the gap.

Quick rules of thumb, and where they fall short

  • The 4% rule: withdraw 4% of your savings in the first year and raise it with inflation. It comes from US market history over 30-year retirements. It implies a target of 25 times the yearly income you need from savings.
  • Save 15% of income: a common target for someone who starts in their twenties, including employer contributions.
  • Income replacement of 70% to 80%: many people spend less after they stop work because commuting, saving and often housing costs fall.

These are starting points. A retirement of 35 years, lower returns or high inflation all need a bigger pot, which is why this calculator lets you set each of those yourself.

How to close a retirement gap

  • Save more, starting now. Money saved at 35 has thirty years to grow. The same amount saved at 55 has ten.
  • Take the full employer match. It is part of your pay.
  • Work a little longer. Each extra year adds contributions, adds growth and removes a year of withdrawals. Try raising the retirement age by two years to see the effect.
  • Keep costs low. Fund fees come straight out of your return.
  • Plan for a long life. Planning to 90 or 95 is prudent. Running out at 85 is a worse outcome than leaving some money unspent.

Retirement questions

How much do I need to retire?

A common estimate is 25 times the yearly income you need from savings, after subtracting pensions. To draw 40,000 a year from savings, that is about 1,000,000 in today’s money. This calculator refines it using your own return, inflation and time horizon.

What is the 4% rule?

It suggests withdrawing 4% of your savings in the first year of retirement and increasing that amount with inflation each year. Historically this lasted at least 30 years in the US. It is a guideline, not a guarantee.

How much should I save each month for retirement?

A typical target is 15% of gross income including any employer match. Enter your numbers above: if there is a gap, the result shows the extra monthly amount that closes it.

Does this calculator account for inflation?

Yes. Your income goal is entered in today’s money and increased with inflation up to and through retirement. The result also shows your savings at retirement in today’s money.

What return should I assume?

A diversified portfolio weighted towards shares has returned about 6% to 8% a year before inflation over long periods. Many planners use a lower return after retirement, such as 4% to 5%, because the portfolio holds more bonds and cash.

Should I include my state pension or Social Security?

Yes. Enter the yearly amount you expect in today’s money. It reduces the income your savings must provide.
