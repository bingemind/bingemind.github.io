How to calculate hours on a time card

For each day, take the clock-out time, subtract the clock-in time, and subtract any unpaid break. Then add up the days. Payroll needs the total in decimal hours, so divide any leftover minutes by 60.

Daily hours = clock-out − clock-in − unpaid break
Decimal hours = hours + (minutes ÷ 60)
Pay = regular hours × rate + overtime hours × rate × 1.5

Example

8:30 AM to 5:15 PM is 8 hours 45 minutes. Take off a 30-minute lunch and you get 8:15, which is 8.25 decimal hours. Five days like that make 41.25 hours: 40 regular and 1.25 overtime. At $20 an hour that is $800 + $37.50 = $837.50.

Rounding, breaks and overnight shifts explained

Most mistakes on a time card come from three places: minutes that are written as if they were decimals, unpaid breaks that are left in or taken out twice, and shifts that cross midnight. This guide covers each one so your total matches what payroll will pay.

Minutes are not decimals

An hour has 60 minutes, so 7:30 is 7.5 hours, not 7.30. A shift from 8:45 to 5:15 is 8 hours 30 minutes, which is 8.5 hours. To convert, divide the minutes by 60: 20 minutes is 0.33 hours, 40 minutes is 0.67 hours. Pay is then hours times rate, so 8.5 hours at $18.00 an hour is $153.00. The chart above shows every minute value so you do not have to divide each time.

Unpaid lunch and other breaks

Enter the length of any unpaid break and it is subtracted from that day. A 30-minute lunch on a 9-hour span leaves 8.5 paid hours. Short rest breaks of about 5 to 20 minutes are generally paid under US federal rules, so they stay in the total. Meal breaks of 30 minutes or more are generally unpaid only when the worker is fully relieved of duty. Rules differ by country and state, and your employer's policy decides what you are actually paid, so check your contract or handbook.

Shifts that go past midnight

For a night shift such as 10:00 PM to 6:00 AM, the clock-out time is earlier than the clock-in time. The calculator treats that as a shift that ends the next day, so it counts 8 hours instead of a negative number. Pay for the whole shift is normally counted in the workweek where it started, but check how your payroll assigns hours that fall on two calendar days.

Rounding to the quarter hour

Many employers round punches to the nearest 5 minutes, 6 minutes (a tenth of an hour) or 15 minutes. In the US, rounding is allowed when it is neutral and averages out so that workers are paid for all the time they work. Always rounding down in the employer's favour is not allowed. Use the rounding setting to match your employer's system and compare the result with your pay stub. If the two totals differ by more than a few minutes a week, ask your payroll team how punches are rounded.

From weekly hours to overtime

After you total the week, hours above the overtime threshold are paid at a higher rate. In the US the federal rule is time and a half after 40 hours in a workweek for most hourly workers. Some states, such as California, also pay overtime after 8 hours in a day. Other countries use different thresholds, which the rule list above covers. If you want the pay for the overtime part worked out in detail, use the overtime calculator and carry your weekly total across.

Weekly, bi-weekly and semi-monthly pay periods

A weekly card covers 7 days. A bi-weekly card covers 14 days, and overtime is still counted week by week, not across the two weeks. A semi-monthly period runs twice a month, for example the 1st to the 15th and the 16th to the end of the month, so the number of working days changes from period to period. Pick the period length that matches your employer, and fill each week separately so overtime is counted correctly.

Checking your time card before you sign

  • Make sure every clock-in has a clock-out, and that AM and PM are right. A missing PM adds 12 hours.
  • Check that the unpaid break is entered once, on the right days.
  • Compare the total with the hours on your pay stub. If they differ, the cause is usually rounding, an unpaid break, or a punch that was edited.
  • Keep your own record. In the US, employers must keep time records, but your own notes help if there is ever a dispute.

Common time card mistakes

Writing 7:30 as 7.30 shortchanges you by 12 minutes. Subtracting a lunch break from a day that already excludes it counts it twice. Adding up daily rounded numbers instead of rounding each punch can shift the total by a few minutes. Forgetting that a shift crosses midnight can produce a total of zero or a negative number. The calculator avoids all four, but it only knows what you enter, so check the times against your actual clock records.

Worked time card examples

These examples use the US federal overtime rule (time and a half after 40 hours a week) and a rate of $20 an hour. Enter the same times above to check them.

Example 1: a standard week

Monday to Friday, 9:00 AM to 5:30 PM with a 30-minute unpaid lunch. Each day is 8 hours, so the week is 40 hours with no overtime. Pay is 40 × $20 = $800.00.

Example 2: a long week with overtime

Monday to Friday, 8:00 AM to 6:00 PM with a one-hour unpaid lunch. Each day is 9 hours, so the week is 45 hours. The first 40 hours pay $800.00. The 5 overtime hours pay 5 × $30 = $150.00. Gross pay is $950.00.

Example 3: a night shift

Monday to Friday, 10:00 PM to 6:00 AM with a 30-minute unpaid break. Each shift is 7.5 hours, so the week is 37.5 hours. At $22 an hour, pay is 37.5 × $22 = $825.00.

Example 4: a split shift

On Saturday you work 9:00 AM to 1:00 PM, go home, and return from 5:00 PM to 9:00 PM. The two blocks are 4 hours each, so the day is 8 hours. Enter the gap as an unpaid break of 4 hours, or enter each block as its own row.

Example 5: how rounding changes a day

You clock in at 8:08 AM and out at 4:52 PM with a 30-minute lunch. Exact time is 8 hours 14 minutes, or 8.23 hours. With 15-minute rounding the punches become 8:15 and 4:45, which is 8 hours 0 minutes after the lunch. One day can move by a quarter hour either way, and rounding is fair only when it averages out over many shifts.

Working-time rules outside the US

Overtime and rest rules vary widely. In the EU, the Working Time Directive sets an average maximum of 48 hours a week and minimum rest periods, and each country adds its own overtime pay rules. In the UK there is no statutory overtime rate, so it depends on your contract. In India, the Factories Act generally limits work to 48 hours a week, with overtime at twice the ordinary rate for covered workers, and state laws for shops and offices differ. In Australia, awards and enterprise agreements set penalty rates. The rule list in the calculator applies a typical threshold for each country, and you can switch to custom rules if your contract is different.

Time card tips for employers and freelancers

For small employers

Pick one clear rule for rounding, breaks and the start of the workweek, write it down, and apply it to everyone. Make sure employees can see and approve their hours before payroll runs. Keep time records for the period your local law requires. In the US, the Fair Labor Standards Act requires employers to keep records of hours worked and wages paid, generally for at least three years for payroll records. Check state requirements, which can be longer.

For freelancers and contractors

Log your time as you go, since estimating at the end of the week is usually wrong. Total each client separately, and multiply by your rate for the invoice. Consider billing in 15-minute or 6-minute blocks and say so in your contract. The CSV download opens in any spreadsheet so you can attach it to an invoice as proof of hours.

For nannies, carers and household staff

Household employees are covered by wage rules in many places, including overtime in some US states. Keep a weekly card signed by both sides. Count unpaid breaks only when the employee is truly free to leave or rest, and be clear about overnight stays and on-call time, which are treated differently by different states and countries.

Converting a total into decimal hours for payroll software

Most payroll systems want decimal hours, such as 38.75, and not 38 hours 45 minutes. The result panel shows both. If you enter hours manually, round to two decimals so that the difference over a year stays small. Use the minutes chart above to convert quickly.

Frequently overlooked details

Daylight saving changes can add or remove an hour in a night shift on the days the clocks move. Time zones matter for remote teams, so record the local time of the place where the work is done. Public holidays may pay at a higher rate in your region. Lastly, always confirm the final total against your employer's own timekeeping system, since that is what is used to pay you.

Time card questions

How do I calculate hours worked on a time card?

For each day, subtract the clock-in time from the clock-out time, then subtract unpaid breaks. Add the days together for the week. For example, 8:30 AM to 5:15 PM is 8 hours 45 minutes; minus a 30-minute lunch gives 8 hours 15 minutes, or 8.25 decimal hours.

How do I convert minutes to decimal hours?

Divide the minutes by 60. 15 minutes is 0.25 hours, 30 minutes is 0.5, 45 minutes is 0.75. So 7 hours 45 minutes is 7.75 decimal hours. Payroll uses decimal hours because you can multiply them by your hourly rate.

Are lunch breaks paid?

Under federal law, meal breaks of 30 minutes or more where you are fully relieved of duty can be unpaid. Short rest breaks of about 5 to 20 minutes count as work time and must be paid. Some states have their own meal and rest break rules.

How do I calculate an overnight shift?

If your clock-out time is earlier than your clock-in time, the shift crossed midnight. Add 24 hours to the clock-out time before subtracting. This calculator does that for you, so 10:00 PM to 6:30 AM with a 30-minute break is 8 hours.

Is time card rounding legal?

Federal rules allow rounding to the nearest 5 minutes, tenth of an hour (6 minutes) or quarter hour (15 minutes), as long as it is neutral and does not always favor the employer over time. California courts are stricter, so many employers there pay exact minutes.

How is overtime calculated on a bi-weekly time card?

Overtime is counted for each workweek on its own, even if you are paid every two weeks. 50 hours in week one and 30 in week two gives 10 overtime hours, not zero. This calculator splits bi-weekly cards into two weeks for that reason.
