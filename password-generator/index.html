    How the passwords are made

    Each character or word is chosen with crypto.getRandomValues, the cryptographically secure random number generator built into every modern browser. It is the same source used for encryption keys. The generator uses rejection sampling, so every character is exactly equally likely, and when you pick several character types it guarantees at least one of each.

    Nothing is sent over the network, logged or stored. Only your settings are remembered in this browser.

    What "bits of entropy" means

    Random password: bits = length × log₂(number of possible characters)
    Passphrase: bits = number of words × log₂(words in the list)

    A 16-character password using upper and lower case letters, numbers and 25 symbols draws from 87 characters: 16 × log₂(87) = 103 bits. Each extra bit doubles the number of guesses an attacker needs.

    How strong is strong enough?

    EntropyRatingGood for
    Under 40 bitsVery weakNothing important
    40 to 59 bitsWeakLow-value accounts with rate limits and 2FA
    60 to 79 bitsGoodMost website accounts
    80 to 99 bitsStrongEmail, banking, password manager vault
    100+ bitsVery strongEncryption keys, Wi-Fi, files that could be attacked offline

    The crack times assume an attacker must try half of all possibilities on average. Online attacks against a live login are slow because sites limit attempts. Offline attacks on stolen password hashes can run at billions of guesses a second on graphics cards, which is why long, random passwords matter.

    These figures only hold for truly random passwords like the ones made here. A password you invent, such as a word with a number on the end, is far weaker than its length suggests, because attackers try those patterns first.

    Passwords or passphrases?

    A passphrase is several random words, such as Blimp-Bless-Turf-Joy4-Poet. It is easier to remember and type, especially on a phone or TV. This generator uses the EFF short word list of 1,296 common words, so each word adds 10.3 bits. Five words plus a digit give about 57 bits, six words about 62 bits, and eight words about 83 bits.

    A random password packs more strength into fewer characters, which is ideal when a password manager types it for you.

    Good habits

    • Use a different password for every account. Reused passwords are the main way accounts are taken over after another site is breached.
    • Use a password manager. Built-in managers in Apple, Google and Microsoft devices, or dedicated apps, remember everything so you only need one strong passphrase.
    • Turn on two-factor authentication, ideally an authenticator app or passkey rather than SMS.
    • Prefer passkeys where a site offers them. They cannot be phished or reused.
    • Do not change strong passwords on a schedule. Change them when there is a sign of compromise. This is current advice from NIST and the UK's NCSC.

    When a site rejects your password

    • Too long: some older systems cap passwords at 16 or 20 characters. Lower the length.
    • Symbol not allowed: switch symbols off and add 3 or 4 characters of length to make up the strength.
    • Must contain a symbol: passphrases can use "." or "_" as the separator.
    • Hard to read aloud or type on a TV: tick "Avoid look-alikes" or use a passphrase.

    Password questions

    Is this password generator safe?

    Yes. Passwords are created in your browser using its cryptographic random generator and are never sent, logged or stored. You can even disconnect from the internet after the page loads and it still works.

    How long should a password be?

    At least 12 characters for everyday accounts and 16 or more for important ones such as email and banking. With a password manager there is no reason not to use 20 or more.

    What makes a password strong?

    Length and randomness. A long password chosen at random from a large set of characters, used on only one account, is strong. Patterns, names, dates and dictionary words make it weak.

    Are passphrases secure?

    Yes, if the words are chosen at random. Five random words from a 1,296-word list give about 52 bits, six give about 62, and seven or more are recommended for important accounts.

    What is password entropy?

    A measure of how unpredictable a password is, in bits. Each bit doubles the number of guesses needed. 80 bits or more is considered strong for most uses.

    Should I change my passwords regularly?

    Current NIST and NCSC guidance says no, unless there is a reason to think a password has been exposed. Forced changes tend to produce weaker, predictable passwords.
