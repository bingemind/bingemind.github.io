How loan payments are calculated

Most personal, car, student and business loans are amortizing: you pay the same amount every period, and each payment covers the interest for that period plus some of the balance.

Payment = P × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)−n)
P = amount borrowed · r = yearly rate ÷ payments per year · n = number of payments

Example: 20,000 at 10% for 5 years with monthly payments. r = 0.10 ÷ 12 = 0.008333 and n = 60, so the payment is 424.94. Over five years you pay 25,496.45, of which 5,496.45 is interest.

Interest rate and APR are not the same

The interest rate is what the lender charges on the balance. The APR also counts fees you pay to get the loan. If a lender takes a 3% origination fee out of a 20,000 loan, you receive 19,400 but repay as if you borrowed 20,000. This calculator finds the rate that makes your payments match the money you actually received. That figure is the one to compare between lenders.

How the term changes what you pay

A longer term lowers the payment but raises the total interest, because you owe the money for longer. For 20,000 at 10%:

Going from 3 to 7 years almost halves the payment and more than doubles the interest. The "Compare loan terms" table in the result does this for your own numbers.

Biweekly and weekly payments

If you are paid every two weeks, matching the loan to your pay cycle makes budgeting easier. Paying every two weeks also means 26 payments a year, so slightly more principal goes in earlier and a little less interest builds up.

Ways to pay less for a loan

  • Compare APR, not the headline rate. A lower rate with a high fee can cost more than a higher rate with no fee.
  • Pay a little extra. Extra money goes straight to the balance. Even a small amount each payment cuts months off. Check that your lender has no early repayment charge.
  • Choose the shortest term you can afford comfortably. Leave room in your budget for emergencies, because a missed payment costs more than the interest you save.
  • Improve your credit before you apply. Paying down card balances and correcting errors on your credit report can move you into a cheaper rate band.
  • Be careful with add-ons. Payment protection insurance and similar products are often financed into the loan, so you pay interest on them too.

Fixed and variable rates

A fixed rate keeps the same payment for the whole term. A variable or floating rate follows a benchmark, so the payment can rise or fall. To stress-test a variable loan, run the calculator again with the rate 2 points higher and make sure you could still pay.

Loan questions

How do I calculate a loan payment?

Multiply the amount borrowed by the periodic rate and divide by 1 minus (1 + rate) to the power of minus the number of payments. For 20,000 at 10% over 5 years the monthly payment is 424.94.

What is the difference between interest rate and APR?

The interest rate is charged on the balance. APR adds the lender’s fees, so it shows the full yearly cost. A 10% loan with a 3% upfront fee over 5 years has an APR of about 11.3%.

Is EMI the same as a monthly payment?

Yes. EMI, or equated monthly instalment, is the term used in India and some other countries for a fixed monthly loan payment. It uses the same amortization formula.

Does paying extra reduce the interest?

Yes, as long as the lender applies the extra to principal. The balance falls faster, so less interest is charged each period and the loan ends early. Ask whether there is an early repayment fee first.

Should I choose a longer term for a lower payment?

Only if you need the lower payment. A longer term costs more in total. If you take the longer term for safety, you can still pay extra when money allows and finish early.

Can I use this for a student or business loan?

Yes, for any loan with a fixed rate and level payments. It does not model interest-only periods, grace periods or income-driven repayment plans.
