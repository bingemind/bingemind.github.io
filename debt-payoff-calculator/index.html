How the payoff plan works

Each month, interest is added to every balance. You pay the minimum on every debt, then put all the extra money on one target debt. When a debt is cleared, its payment is not spent: it rolls onto the next target. Your total monthly budget stays the same until everything is paid.

Two ways to choose the target

  • Avalanche: highest interest rate first. This always costs the least interest.
  • Snowball: smallest balance first. You clear individual debts sooner, which many people find keeps them going.
  • Your order: the debts are paid in the order you list them. Use it when a debt matters more than its rate suggests, such as one owed to family or one with a promotional rate about to end.

The result compares your choice with the main alternative, so you can see what the motivation of the snowball costs you in interest. Often the difference is small. The table lists the month each debt is cleared and the interest paid on it.

Why minimum payments keep you in debt

Card issuers usually set the minimum at around 1% to 3% of the balance plus interest. As the balance falls, so does the minimum, so the debt shrinks very slowly.

A 5,000 card balance at 24% with a 2.5% minimum takes more than 20 years to clear if you only ever pay the minimum, and costs more in interest than the original debt. Fixing the payment at the first month's amount instead, about 125, clears it in under 7 years. Adding 100 on top brings it down to about 2 and a half years.

The line "Minimums only would take" in the result shows your own version of this, using your current minimums as fixed payments.

Ways to speed things up

  • Balance transfer cards: a 0% promotional rate lets every payment reduce the balance. Count the transfer fee, typically 3% to 5%, and plan to clear the debt before the offer ends.
  • Debt consolidation loan: one loan at a lower fixed rate replaces several cards. It only helps if the new rate is lower and you stop using the cards.
  • Ask for a lower rate: lenders sometimes agree, particularly for customers with a good payment record.
  • Put windfalls on the target debt: tax refunds, bonuses and sales of unused things.
  • Keep a small emergency fund so an unexpected bill does not go straight back on a card.

If you cannot keep up

If the minimums alone are more than you can pay, contact your lenders early and get free, regulated debt advice: a non-profit credit counsellor (US, Canada), StepChange or National Debtline (UK), the National Debt Helpline (Australia), or MABS (Ireland). Avoid companies that charge upfront fees to "settle" your debt.

Which debts to include

Include credit cards, store cards, personal loans, car loans, student loans, overdrafts and buy-now-pay-later balances. Most people leave their mortgage out, because its rate is low and the term is long. Use the mortgage calculator to test extra payments on that.

Some debts deserve to jump the queue whatever their rate: anything in arrears, tax debts, and loans with a guarantor or secured on something you need.

Debt payoff questions

Is the snowball or avalanche method better?

Avalanche, paying the highest rate first, always costs the least interest. Snowball, paying the smallest balance first, gives quicker wins. The best method is the one you will stick to, and this calculator shows the cost difference between them.

How do I work out my debt-free date?

Add interest to each balance monthly, subtract your payments, and repeat until every balance reaches zero. The calculator does this for all your debts together and shows the month you finish.

How much faster will I be debt-free if I pay extra?

It depends on your rates, but the effect is large. Try the extra payment field: on typical card debt, an extra 100 a month can cut the payoff time by more than half.

Should I pay off debt or save first?

Build a small emergency fund first, then pay down any debt charging more than you could earn on savings. Card interest of 20% or more outweighs almost any savings return.

Does paying off debt improve my credit score?

Usually yes. Lower balances reduce your credit utilisation, and a record of on-time payments is the biggest factor in most scoring models.

What happens to a payment when a debt is cleared?

It rolls over to the next debt in the plan. That is why progress speeds up over time: the amount going to the target debt keeps growing.
