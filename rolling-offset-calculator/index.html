What is a rolling offset?

A rolling offset moves a run of pipe or conduit up and sideways at the same time. The run ends on a new line that is parallel to the old one. You need it when a beam, a duct, a wall or another pipe is in the way and the path is not flat or square. This rolling offset calculator gives you every number you need to lay it out: the true offset, the travel, the run and the cut length.

What each number means

  • Rise is the vertical distance between the two center lines.
  • Roll is the sideways distance between the two center lines.
  • True offset (the set) is the straight distance between the center lines. It is the diagonal across the rise and the roll.
  • Travel is the center to center length of the angled piece between the two fittings.
  • Run is how far the offset moves along the pipe. It is the length the offset uses up on the straight line.

How to do a rolling offset with the calculator

  1. Measure the rise and the roll from center line to center line, in inches.
  2. Type them in. Use decimals such as 12.5, or fractions such as 12 1/2. You can also type feet and inches, such as 2'-3 1/2". The fraction buttons set the fraction on the field you used last.
  3. Pick the fitting angle. For pipe: 22.5, 45 or 60 degrees. For conduit: 10, 22.5, 30, 45 or 60 degrees. You can also type your own.
  4. For pipe, pick the pipe type and size to subtract the fitting take-off. The tool then shows the cut length.
  5. For EMT, switch to conduit mode and pick the bend angle. The tool shows the distance between bends and the shrink.

Which fitting angle should you use?

Most plumbers use 45 degree fittings. They are easy to stock, and they keep the offset short. A 22.5 degree fitting makes a long, gentle offset that is good for drain lines. A 60 degree fitting makes a short, steep offset when space is tight, but it slows the flow more. A smaller angle always makes a longer travel for the same offset. Try each angle in the tool and compare the run.

Rolling offset formula

The math is right-triangle geometry. Below is the formula for a rolling offset. The calculator follows the same steps. For shrink and the fitting roll angle, see the full rolling offset formula page.

  • True offset = the square root of (rise squared + roll squared)
  • Travel = true offset / sin(angle)
  • Run = true offset / tan(angle)
  • Cut length = travel - 2 x take-off

Rolling offset example in pipe

Here is a rolling offset pipe job from start to finish. The steps show how to calculate a rolling offset by hand.

A copper line must clear a duct. The rise is 12 inches and the roll is 9 inches. You use 45 degree fittings.

  1. True offset = the square root of (144 + 81) = 15 inches.
  2. Travel = 15 / sin 45 = 21.21 inches, or about 21 3/16 inches.
  3. Run = 15 / tan 45 = 15 inches.
  4. The 3/4 inch copper 45 degree fitting has a take-off of 11/32 inch (0.344). Cut length = 21.21 - 2 x 0.344 = 20.53 inches, or about 20 1/2 inches.

Rolling offset in EMT conduit

Conduit is bent, not fitted, so the rules change a little. The bender angle sets two numbers. The multiplier is one divided by the sine of the angle. Multiply the true offset by it to get the distance between the two bends. Shrink is the length the conduit loses when you bend it. It equals the true offset times the tangent of half the angle.

How to bend a rolling offset in EMT

Both bends of a rolling offset lie in one plane. That plane is tilted from the rise (vertical) direction by an angle that depends on the rise and the roll. The tool shows it as "Bend plane tilt from rise".

  1. Measure the rise and the roll from center line to center line.
  2. Switch the tool to EMT conduit and pick the bender angle.
  3. Read the distance between bends, the total shrink and the bend plane tilt.
  4. Mark the first bend on the conduit. Mark the second bend the distance between bends from the first one.
  5. Make bend 1 with the bend plane at the tilt shown.
  6. Turn the conduit 180 degrees, so the second bend lies in the same plane, and make bend 2 at the second mark.
  7. Add the shrink to your overall length before you cut, then check the shape against the job.

Tips for a clean job

  • Measure to the center of the pipe, not to the edge. For conduit, measure to the center of the tube.
  • Use the same fitting angle on both ends of the offset.
  • Check the take-off against the fittings in your hand. Sizes differ between makers.
  • Dry fit the piece before you glue, solder or weld.
  • If the cut length comes out zero or negative, the fittings are too large for that offset. Use a smaller angle or a longer rise and roll.

Frequently asked questions

What is a rolling offset?

A rolling offset moves a pipe or conduit run both up (the rise) and sideways (the roll) at the same time, so it ends on a new parallel line. You use it to clear a beam, a duct, a wall or another pipe in a tight space.

How do you calculate a rolling offset?

First find the true offset: the square root of rise squared plus roll squared. Then divide the true offset by the sine of the fitting angle to get the travel. For a rise of 12 inches, a roll of 9 inches and 45 degree fittings, the true offset is 15 inches and the travel is 21.21 inches.

What is the formula for a rolling offset?

The formula for rolling offset travel is the square root of (rise squared + roll squared), divided by the sine of the fitting angle. The page on the rolling offset formula lists every step, including run, shrink and cut length.

What is the difference between travel and run?

Travel is the length of the angled piece, measured center to center between the two fittings. Run is how far that piece moves forward along the straight line of the pipe. The run equals the true offset divided by the tangent of the fitting angle.

How do I find the cut length of the pipe?

Subtract the take-off of each fitting from the travel. Take-off is the part of the fitting that does not count toward the pipe, which is the center to the bottom of the socket for glued or soldered fittings. Pick a pipe type and size in the tool and it does the subtraction for you. The table values come from maker catalogs for 45 degree fittings. For other angles, type your own take-off.

How do I use this for EMT conduit?

Switch to EMT conduit. Pick the bend angle and the tool shows the multiplier, the distance between bends, the shrink and the tilt of the bend plane. Both bends lie in that one plane. Make the first bend, turn the conduit 180 degrees, and make the second bend at the second mark.

What is shrink and why does it matter?

When you bend an offset, the conduit gets shorter along its length. That loss is the shrink. For each inch of offset it is about 3/8 inch at 45 degrees, 1/4 inch at 30 degrees and 3/16 inch at 22.5 degrees. Add the total shrink to your length before you cut.

Which fitting angle should I use?

Use 45 degrees for most pipe work because 45 degree fittings are easy to find and give a short run. Use 22.5 degrees for a long, gentle offset. Use 60 degrees when you need a short, steep offset. In conduit, 30 degrees and 45 degrees are the most common bender angles.

Does this work on my phone?

Yes. The buttons are large, the answers update as you type, and the fraction buttons set 1/8 inch steps without a full keyboard, and you can type feet and inches. Nothing you type leaves your device.

What is the multiplier for a 45 degree rolling offset?

It is 1.414. Multiply the true offset by it to get the travel, or the distance between bends in conduit. A 30 degree bend uses 2.0, and a 22.5 degree bend uses 2.613.

How do I measure rise and roll?

Measure from the center line of the first pipe or conduit to the center line of the second. The rise is the vertical distance. The roll is the sideways distance. Measure to the center, not to the edge.
