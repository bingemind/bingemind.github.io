How a car payment is calculated

First work out the amount you finance, then apply the standard loan formula.

Amount financed = price − down payment − trade-in + amount still owed on the trade-in + tax and fees (if added to the loan)
Monthly payment = financed × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)−n), where r = APR ÷ 12

Example: a 35,000 car, 5,000 down, 7% sales tax (2,450) and 600 in fees, financed over 60 months at 7.8%. The amount financed is 33,050 and the payment is 666.98 a month. Total interest is about 6,969.

Sales tax and the trade-in credit

In most US states, sales tax is charged on the price minus your trade-in, which is how this calculator works it out. A few states tax the full price. Outside the US, tax such as VAT or GST is usually already in the advertised price, so the tax field starts at 0 for those countries.

Loan length: the choice that costs the most

Dealers often quote a payment, not a price. Stretching the loan makes any car look affordable, but the interest grows quickly and the car loses value faster than you pay it off.

The 84-month loan costs more than twice the interest of the 36-month loan. A common guideline is the 20/4/10 rule: put 20% down, finance for no more than 4 years, and keep all car costs under 10% of your gross income.

Trade-ins, negative equity and other traps

  • Negative equity: if you owe more on your current car than it is worth, the difference is rolled into the new loan. Enter it under "Still owed on trade-in". You then pay interest on a car you no longer own.
  • Dealer add-ons: extended warranties, paint protection and gap insurance are often added to the loan. Price them separately and decide on each one.
  • 0% offers: these usually need excellent credit and may replace a cash rebate. Compare the rebate with the interest you would save.
  • Balloon and PCP deals (common in the UK and Australia): the monthly figure is low because a large final payment is left at the end. This calculator models a standard loan, so use it to compare against the total cost of those deals.
  • Get pre-approved: a quote from your bank or credit union before you visit the dealer gives you a rate to beat.

The full cost of owning a car

The loan is only part of it. Budget for insurance, fuel or charging, servicing, tyres, registration and parking. New cars typically lose 15% to 20% of their value in the first year and around half within five years, which is why a nearly new car is often the cheapest way to drive.

Use the "Total cost of the car" line in the result to compare two cars or two offers on the same basis: it adds what you pay upfront, every loan payment and the value of your trade-in.

Car loan questions

How do I calculate a monthly car payment?

Subtract your down payment and trade-in from the price, add tax and fees if you finance them, then apply the loan formula with the APR divided by 12 and the number of months. 33,050 financed at 7.8% for 60 months is 666.98 a month.

Is a 72 or 84 month car loan a bad idea?

It lowers the payment but costs much more interest, and you are likely to owe more than the car is worth for several years. If you need 84 months to afford the payment, a cheaper car is usually the safer choice.

How much should I put down on a car?

Aim for at least 20% on a new car and 10% on a used one. That offsets first-year depreciation, so you do not owe more than the car is worth.

Is sales tax charged before or after the trade-in?

In most US states the trade-in value is subtracted before sales tax is calculated. California, Hawaii, Maryland and Virginia are among the states that tax the full price, so check your state.

What is negative equity?

It means you owe more on your loan than the car is worth. When you trade the car in, that shortfall is added to your next loan. Enter it as the amount still owed on the trade-in.

Should I finance through the dealer or my bank?

Compare both. Get a pre-approval from a bank or credit union first, then ask the dealer to beat that APR. Compare the APR and the total cost, not just the monthly payment.
