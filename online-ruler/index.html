    Quick start in 3 steps

    1. Calibrate. Press Calibrate at the top right of the page (on a phone, it sits above the ruler). Hold a credit card against the screen and match its edges to the outline. This takes about 20 seconds.
    2. Pick a unit. Choose cm, mm, inches or pixels. You can also show cm and inches together.
    3. Measure. Put your object against the ruler, with one end at the zero mark in the corner. Pull a guide out of the ruler to mark the other end. The length appears at the bottom of the ruler.

    This online ruler works in the browser on a phone, a tablet and a computer. There is nothing to install.

    Why most online rulers are wrong

    A web page cannot see how big your screen is. It sees pixels. A CSS pixel is not a fixed length. The web standard says 96 CSS pixels make one inch, but that is a reference value, not a measurement of your hardware.

    Real screens differ. A phone packs many pixels into a small panel. A laptop may scale its display to make text easier to read. A monitor may be set to a lower resolution than its panel supports. Each of these changes how long one pixel is in real life.

    Many online rulers still draw at 96 pixels per inch and call it done. On a typical laptop that makes the ruler too short, and on a phone it can be far off. An actual size ruler needs one fact from you: how big something real looks on your screen. That is what calibration does.

    Until you calibrate, this ruler shows a labeled estimate. It never says "calibrated" for a guess.

    How to read cm, mm and inches

    On a metric ruler, the numbered marks are centimeters. The small marks between them are millimeters. There are 10 mm in 1 cm. To read a length, find the last numbered mark, then count the small marks past it.

    An inch ruler is split into halves, quarters, eighths and sixteenths. The longer the mark, the larger the fraction. Count the small marks past the last whole inch, then reduce: 8 sixteenths is 1/2, and 10 sixteenths is 5/8. The chart below shows each step with its decimal and millimeter value.

    What people measure with a screen ruler

    A screen ruler suits small, flat things that you can hold against the glass. People use it for:

    • Screws, bolts, washers and other small parts, to match a size.
    • Beads, gemstones and jewelry parts, in millimeters.
    • Photos, prints and stamps, to check the size before ordering a frame.
    • Label and sticker sizes.
    • Spacing on a design, when you want to see a layout at real size.

    Hold the object flat and close to the glass. If it sits high above the screen, the angle of view makes it look larger or smaller than it is. Look straight at the edge you are reading.

    A worked example: measuring a screw

    Say you want the length of a wood screw. Here is the full routine, from start to finish.

    1. Calibrate with a card and run the check step. The status chip should read "Calibrated, checked".
    2. Choose millimeters. Screw sizes are often given in mm, such as 4 x 40.
    3. Lay the screw flat on the glass, below the top ruler. Put the flat of the head against the left ruler, or against a guide set at zero.
    4. Press Add guide, then drag the guide to the tip of the screw. The readout shows the length, for example "40 mm = 4 cm = 1 9/16 in".
    5. Look straight down at the tip while you place the guide. If you look from the side, the guide appears to move.

    Measure twice, and take the middle value if the two differ. A screen shows whole pixels, so a reading is only as fine as the pixel grid on your screen. On most phones the grid is finer than a tenth of a millimeter. On a standard monitor one pixel is about a quarter of a millimeter.

    Common mistakes to avoid

    • Skipping the calibration. The first screen is an estimate. Use the card step before you rely on a number.
    • Changing the zoom afterwards. Chrome, Edge and Firefox keep the scale when you zoom the page, but Safari and display scaling changes can break it. If in doubt, calibrate again.
    • Using a bent or worn card. A card with a warped edge is hard to line up. Pick a flat one. A coin is a good second check, because its edge is a clean circle.
    • Looking from an angle. The glass has a small gap above the pixels. A sideways view moves the object relative to the marks. Look straight down.
    • Measuring something thick. A tall object casts the edge away from the screen. Measure flat things, or measure at the base.
    • Trusting a stretched screen. If your monitor is set to a resolution with a different shape than the panel, everything is stretched. Use the native resolution, or calibrate with the card, which shows the true size of what you see.

    Limits you should know

    • Length. The ruler can only be as long as your screen. A 6.1 inch phone has a screen about 14 cm tall, so it fits small objects. A laptop screen is wider, from about 29 to 34 cm. For long objects, use a real ruler or a tape measure.
    • Edges. The rulers touch the real glass edge only in full screen. Press F, or the Full screen button, so the rulers use the whole display. Phones in Safari may not offer full screen.
    • Zoom. The ruler stores the physical size of a screen pixel, so page zoom is designed not to change the real size in Chrome, Edge and Firefox. We have not tested every browser and screen. In Safari, keep zoom at 100 percent (press Cmd and 0).
    • More than one screen. A calibration belongs to one screen. If you move the window to a second monitor, the tool tells you and asks you to calibrate it or use a saved one.
    • Curved glass. A thick bezel or curved edge can hide a little of the screen at the sides.

    Privacy

    Everything runs in your browser. The tool does not send your measurements or your calibration anywhere. Your calibration is saved in your browser, one small record for each screen, so you do not have to do it again. You can remove it with Reset calibration in the Options. If your browser blocks storage, the ruler still works, and you calibrate again next time.

    Frequently asked questions

    How accurate is an online ruler?

    It depends on the calibration. Before you calibrate, the ruler is a guess based on your screen type, and the size can be well off. After you match a credit card to the outline, the result depends on how closely you line up the edges. Zoom in, use the 0.1 mm buttons, and run the check step with a coin.

    How do I calibrate an online ruler with a credit card?

    Press Calibrate at the top right of the page (on a phone, above the ruler), then choose the Card tab. Hold a card flat against the screen and line up one edge with the outline. Drag the grip on the right edge until the other edge matches. Use the 0.1 mm buttons for fine changes, then press "My card matches". Then check the result with a coin.

    How do I change inches to cm on the online ruler?

    One inch is exactly 2.54 cm. Pull a guide out to the end of your object. The tool shows the length in your chosen unit and in the other units too, so an inch reading also appears in cm and mm. You can also divide mm by 25.4 to get inches.

    Why does the ruler look too big or too small?

    Most rulers assume 96 pixels per inch. Many screens use a different density, and browsers and operating systems scale the page. The result is a ruler that is too long or too short. Calibrate once and the ruler uses the real size of a pixel on your screen.

    Does browser zoom or display scaling change the ruler?

    The ruler stores the physical size of a screen pixel, so page zoom should not change its real size in Chrome, Edge and Firefox. We have not tested every browser and setup. In Safari, and after you change the display scaling, keep zoom at 100 percent or calibrate again.

    Can I use my phone as a ruler? How long can it measure?

    Yes. Open this page on the phone and calibrate. The ruler can only be as long as the screen. A 6.1 inch phone has a screen about 14 cm tall, so it measures small objects. Turn the phone sideways to use the longer edge.

    Can I use my phone camera as a ruler?

    Not here. A camera ruler needs a reference object of known size in the same photo, which is a different tool. This ruler measures flat objects that you hold against the screen.

    Is the ruler true to size on a Mac, Windows PC, Chromebook and iPad?

    It can be, on all of them, after you calibrate. The screens differ in pixel density and scaling, so the page cannot know the real size without your help. A saved calibration is kept per screen, so a laptop and an external monitor each keep their own.

    Is my calibration saved? Is my data private?

    The calibration is saved in your browser only, as one small record per screen. Nothing you measure is sent to a server. If your browser blocks storage, the ruler still works, but you will calibrate again on the next visit.

    Is it free? Do I need to install an app?

    It is free and runs in the browser. There is nothing to install and no account to make.
