      What a board and batten calculator does

      A board and batten calculator tells you where to put each batten so the gaps on your wall are equal. You give it the wall width, the width of your battens and how many you want. It returns the exact gap, a pencil mark for the center of every batten, and the boards to buy. This board and batten spacing calculator also draws the wall to scale, so you can see the result before you cut anything.

      Equal gaps matter because the eye catches a small difference. If six gaps are 12 inches and the last one is 9, the wall looks wrong, even when no one can say why.

      How to space board and batten on a wall

      1. Measure the wall width in two or three places. Use the smallest number.
      2. Choose your batten width. A 1x2 is 1 1/2 inches wide, a 1x3 is 2 1/2 and a 1x4 is 3 1/2. These are the real sizes, not the names on the label.
      3. Pick the number of battens, or type the gap you like and let the tool choose the count.
      4. Decide if you want a batten at each end of the wall. Most people do, because the end batten hides the joint at the corner.
      5. Copy the marks. Measure from the left edge of the wall and mark the center of each batten at the listed distance.

      The formula

      With a batten at each end, the number of gaps is one less than the number of battens:

      gap = (wall width − battens × batten width) ÷ (battens − 1)

      With a gap at each end instead, add one:

      gap = (wall width − battens × batten width) ÷ (battens + 1)

      The distance from one batten center to the next is the batten width plus the gap. Builders call this the on-center spacing.

      Worked example: a 12 foot wall

      The wall is 144 inches wide. You use 1x3 battens, 2 1/2 inches wide, and want 10 battens with one at each end.

      Ten battens take 25 inches. That leaves 119 inches for 9 gaps. Each gap is 13.22 inches, which is 13 1/4 inches on a tape measure. The on-center spacing is 15 3/4 inches.

      The first center mark is 1 1/4 inches from the left edge. The last one is 11 feet 10 3/4 inches. The tool rounds each mark to the nearest 1/16 inch, so no mark is off by more than 1/32 inch.

      How wide should the gaps be?

      Most board and batten walls use gaps of 8 to 16 inches. Narrow gaps look formal and busy. Wide gaps look modern and calm. A common starting point is 12 inches. Try a few counts with the tool and compare the drawings. The one that feels right on screen will feel right on the wall.

      The wall height and the room matter too. A tall hallway can take wider gaps than a small powder room. Many people also line up the battens with a window or a door. If you want that, mark the window edges first and test the count that puts a batten there.

      Horizontal rails and the boards to buy

      If your design has a top rail, turn on the rails option. The tool spaces them from the floor to the ceiling and adds them to the cut list. Rails are longer than most boards, so the tool splits them and counts the pieces.

      The boards table shows how many 8, 10 and 12 foot boards you need. It allows 1/8 inch for each saw cut and packs the pieces to waste as little as possible. The best option is highlighted. Buy one extra board, because a bad cut or a bowed board happens.

      Board and batten for an accent wall

      An accent wall puts the pattern on one wall only, so the gaps must be equal and the edges must look finished. Gaps of 10 to 14 inches are common. Enter the width and height of that wall and compare a few counts. The accent wall page lists the count and gap for widths from 8 to 16 feet.

      Board and batten for a bathroom

      Bathroom walls are short, so small errors show. Many people use tighter gaps here, 6 to 10 inches, and stop the battens at about 4 feet as a wainscot. Enter that height as the wall height. The bathroom page has a table for 5 to 8 foot walls and the boards to buy for a 4 foot wainscot.

      Board and batten for an entire room

      Measure each wall on its own and run the numbers for each one. Keep the same batten width and a similar gap on every wall, so the room looks even. Start from a corner and work around. If a wall has a window, a door or an outlet, mark those first and check that no batten lands on an edge. Add up the boards from each wall, then buy one extra. The tool works in the browser on a phone or a laptop, so there is no app to install.

      Square and grid layouts

      A square or grid look comes from battens that run up and down and rails that run across. Turn on the rails option and pick how many you want. The tool spaces the battens evenly and puts the rails at even steps from the floor to the ceiling, then adds both to the cut list. See the rails page for the marks. To make the boxes closer to square, choose a batten count whose gap is near the gap between rails.

      Board and batten siding: vinyl, Hardie and wood

      The same math works for board and batten siding on the outside of a house. Vinyl board and batten siding comes in fixed widths, and so does Hardie (fiber cement) siding. Their battens are often wider than a 1x3. Look up the real width of the product you plan to buy, enter it as the batten width and enter the width of the exterior wall. The tool gives the gap and the marks. It does not know the lap, the nailing rules or the board lengths of a siding brand, so follow the maker's install guide for those.

      Inches or centimeters, and free to use

      The calculator is free and needs no sign-up. Use inches, or switch to metric and enter centimeters. Marks and gaps show to the nearest 1/16 inch, or the nearest millimeter in metric.

      Tips before you nail

      • Draw a level line across the wall for the top rail first. Then mark the batten centers along it.
      • Use a plumb line or a laser for each batten. Walls and ceilings are rarely square.
      • Find the studs. Nail the battens into studs where you can, and use construction adhesive between them.
      • Cut the battens after you finish the first one, and check the next against it.
      • Caulk the seams and fill the nail holes before you paint.

      Frequently asked questions

      How far apart should board and batten be?

      Most walls use gaps of 8 to 16 inches between battens. 12 inches is a common choice. A wider gap looks calmer and more modern. A narrower gap looks more formal. Try a few batten counts in the calculator and compare the drawings.

      How do I calculate board and batten spacing?

      Subtract the total width of all battens from the wall width. Divide what is left by the number of gaps. With a batten at each end, the gaps are one fewer than the battens. For a 144 inch wall with ten 2 1/2 inch battens, the gap is (144 - 25) / 9, which is 13 1/4 inches.

      What is on-center spacing?

      It is the distance from the center of one batten to the center of the next. It equals the batten width plus the gap. Marking centers is the easiest way to place battens, because you measure from one fixed point on the wall.

      Should I put a batten at each end of the wall?

      Most people do. A batten in the corner hides the joint and gives the wall a finished edge. Some designers leave a gap at each end instead, so the wall looks as if it floats. The calculator handles both. Switch the option and compare.

      How many boards do I need for a board and batten wall?

      It depends on the wall height and the board length. The calculator packs all your pieces into 8, 10 and 12 foot boards, allows 1/8 inch for each cut, and shows the number for each length. It highlights the one with the fewest boards.

      What is the real width of a 1x3 board?

      A 1x3 is 2 1/2 inches wide and a 1x4 is 3 1/2 inches. A 1x2 is 1 1/2 inches. The name is the size before the wood is dried and planed. Measure your boards with a tape before you enter the width.

      Can I use metric units?

      Yes. Switch to Metric at the top of the tool. Enter sizes in centimeters. Marks and gaps appear to the nearest millimeter, and board lengths change to 2.4, 3.0 and 3.6 meters.

      Is the spacing exact if my wall is not square?

      The math is exact for the width you enter. Walls are often a little off, so measure at the top, middle and bottom and use the smallest width. Mark the batten centers along a level line instead of along the floor.

      Can I use this for vinyl or Hardie board and batten siding?

      You can use it to space the battens on an exterior wall. Enter the real batten width of your vinyl or Hardie product and the wall width. The tool does not set board lengths or fastening, so follow the maker's install guide for those.

      Is the calculator free, and does it work for an accent wall or a bathroom?

      It is free. Enter the width and height of any wall, such as an accent wall or a bathroom wall, in inches or centimeters.
