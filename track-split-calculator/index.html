    What a track split calculator does

    A track split calculator turns a goal time into the time you should hit at the end of every lap. You enter a race distance and a goal. The tool divides the race into laps of 200 m, 300 m, 400 m or any length you set. It then lists the time for each lap and the running total. Coaches use it to set targets. Runners copy it onto a wrist band or a phone note.

    The same tool works the other way. Enter one lap time and it gives your finish time. It can also build an interval session, such as 8 x 400 m at your goal race pace.

    How to use the calculator

    1. Pick what you want: goal to splits, lap to finish, or intervals.
    2. Choose the race distance. The list has the common track and road races. Pick Custom to type your own in meters, kilometers or miles.
    3. Type the goal time, like 20:00 for a 5K. A plain number counts as minutes.
    4. Choose the lap length. A standard outdoor track is 400 m. Many indoor tracks are 200 m.
    5. Pick even, negative or positive pacing. For a negative split, set how many seconds faster the second half should be.
    6. Read the table, then print the pace band or copy the splits.

    The math in one line

    Time per lap = goal time ÷ race distance × lap length. For a 5K in 20:00 on a 400 m track: 1200 s ÷ 5000 m × 400 m = 96 s, or 1:36.0 per lap. The race is 12.5 laps, so the last split covers 200 m and takes 48.0 s.

    One mile is exactly 1609.344 m, by the 1959 international yard and pound agreement. On a 400 m track a mile is 4 laps plus 9.34 m. A mile in 4:00 is 59.7 s per 400 m.

    Even, negative and positive splits

    An even split holds the same pace on every lap. A negative split means the second half of the race is faster than the first. A positive split means it is slower. An even or slightly negative pace is a common plan for races from the mile up, because a start that is too fast often costs more time late in the race than it saves.

    This calculator changes pace in a straight line across the race, so there are no sudden jumps between laps. You set the gap between the two halves. It keeps your total time exact. The tool rounds each running total to the nearest 0.1 s (or 1 s), then takes the lap times as differences, so the laps always add up to your goal.

    Example: 5K in 20:00 with the second half 6 s faster. Lap 1 is 1:36.9, lap 6 is 1:36.1 and the lap at 4,800 m is 1:35.2. The halves are 10:03.0 and 9:57.0.

    Odd distances: read splits from the start or at the line

    Few races are a whole number of laps. A 1500 m race is 3 laps plus 300 m. The 5K is 12 laps plus 200 m. On a real track the odd piece comes first, so every later split falls on the finish line. Choose “At the finish line” to see it that way. Choose “Every lap from the start” if you time yourself with a watch from the gun and want a mark every 400 m.

    Interval sessions at goal pace

    Pick Intervals, enter your goal race and set the repeats. The tool takes the pace of the goal race and shows the time for each repeat. Say your 5K goal is 20:00 and you plan 8 x 400 m with 90 s rest. Each repeat is 1:36.0. The running time is 12:48.0, and the whole session takes 23:18.0 with rest. For repeats longer than a lap, it also lists the split at each lap inside the repeat.

    Lane 1 assumption

    The tool assumes you run in lane 1, and that a lap is exactly the lap length you pick. World Athletics measures a standard 400 m track along a line 30 cm out from the inside edge of lane 1. If you run a whole lap in lane 2, you cover extra distance. Two figures exist, and they depend on how you count. The official rule measures lane 1 at 0.30 m from the curb and every other lane at 0.20 m from the line on its inside edge. With 1.22 m lanes, lane 2 is then 1.12 m further out, so a lap adds 2 × π × 1.12 = 7.04 m. If you simply move out one full lane width of 1.22 m, the geometry gives 2 × π × 1.22 = 7.67 m. At 1:36 per lap that is about 1.7 s and 1.8 s. Add that time to your targets if you cannot stay in lane 1.

    Sources

    • Mile length: 1 mile = 1609.344 m, from the 1959 international yard and pound agreement.
    • Lane width of 1.22 m and where lanes are measured (Rules TR 14.2 and TR 14.4): World Athletics Technical Rules, summarized in Scottish Athletics, Rule of the Month, November 2022.
    • The 7.04 m and 7.67 m figures are our own arithmetic from those distances: circumference = 2 × π × the extra radius. Real tracks vary by a few centimeters.

    Frequently asked questions

    How do I calculate 400 m splits?

    Divide your goal time by the race distance and multiply by 400. For a 5K in 20:00, that is 1200 s divided by 5000 m times 400 m, which is 96 s or 1:36.0 per lap. The last split covers the odd distance: 200 m in 48.0 s.

    What is a negative split?

    A negative split means you run the second half of a race faster than the first half. Set Pacing to Negative, then enter how many seconds faster the second half should be. The calculator keeps your total time and shifts pace in a straight line across the race.

    How many laps is a mile on a track?

    A mile is 1609.344 m. On a 400 m track that is 4 laps plus 9.34 m. A 1600 m race is exactly 4 laps. The calculator shows the odd 9.34 m as its own split.

    What does "at the finish line" mean?

    On a real track, the start of an odd-length race is set back so that you cross the finish line at each later split. Choose At the finish line to put the odd distance first. Choose Every lap from the start to get a mark every lap length from the gun.

    Can I turn one lap time into a finish time?

    Yes. Pick Lap to finish, choose the distance and type your lap time, like 90 or 1:30. The tool assumes the same pace on every lap and shows the finish time with all the splits. A 90 s 400 m lap held for 5000 m gives 18:45.0.

    How do I build an interval workout like 8 x 400?

    Pick Intervals, enter your goal race distance and time, set the repeats and the repeat distance, then add the rest. Each repeat runs at the pace of the goal race. You get the time for each repeat, the running clock with rest, and the total session time.

    Does the calculator assume lane 1?

    Yes. Each lap equals the lap length you pick, measured 30 cm out from the inside edge of lane 1. Running a whole lap in lane 2 adds about 7.0 m by the official measuring lines (lane 1 at 30 cm from the curb, other lanes at 20 cm from the line). Pure geometry, one full 1.22 m lane width, gives 7.7 m. That is 1.7 to 1.8 s per lap at 1:36 pace.

    How do I print a pace band?

    Press Print pace band under the table. Your browser opens its print window with only the band on the page, in black on white. Print it, cut it into strips and tape one to your wrist or the back of your hand.
